## The inverse-square law

Treat the horn as a small (point) source radiating into open air. Its acoustic
power `W` spreads over a sphere of area `4πr²`, so the intensity at distance
`r` is

```text
I(r) = W / (4π r²)
```

Sound pressure level is a log ratio of intensities, `L = 10·log10(I / I0)`.
Comparing two distances `d_ref` and `d`:

```text
L(d) - L(d_ref) = 10·log10( I(d) / I(d_ref) )
                = 10·log10( d_ref² / d² )
                = -20·log10( d / d_ref )
```

So the library uses

```text
L(d) = L_ref - 20·log10(d / d_ref) - a·(d - d_ref) / 1000
```

where `L_ref` is the level at the reference distance `d_ref` and `a` is an
optional air absorption coefficient in dB/km (the distances are converted to
metres for that term). With `a = 0` (the default) this is the pure
inverse-square law.

## 6.02 dB per doubling of distance

Put `d = 2·d_ref` into the formula: the loss is `20·log10(2)`.

```console
$ trainhorn-calc doubling
6.02 dB lost per doubling of distance (free field)

$ trainhorn-calc doubling --json
{
  "db_per_doubling": 6.020599913279624
}
```

The same rule gives 20 dB for every tenfold increase in distance
(`distance_ratio_loss(10)` returns `20.0`). A 150 dB horn (rated at 10 ft)
falls like this:

| Distance (ft) | 10 | 20 | 40 | 80 | 160 | 320 | 640 | 1280 | 2560 | 5120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPL (dB) | 150.00 | 143.98 | 137.96 | 131.94 | 125.92 | 119.90 | 113.88 | 107.86 | 101.84 | 95.81 |

Values from `round(spl_at_distance(150, d), 2)`.

## Reference distance

A dB figure without a distance is meaningless. Horn makers quote levels "at
10 ft", "at 1 m" or sometimes "at 3 ft", and the same horn gives very
different numbers at each. The library's default reference distance is 10 ft;
pass `ref_distance` (`--ref-distance`) and `unit` (`--unit ft|m`) to match the
rating you have.

```console
$ trainhorn-calc spl 120 50 --ref-distance 1 --unit m
86.0 dB at 50 m

$ trainhorn-calc spl 120 3.048 --ref-distance 1 --unit m
110.3 dB at 3.048 m
```

So a horn rated 120 dB at 1 m is only about 110.3 dB at 10 ft (3.048 m):
`distance_ratio_loss(3.048)` is 9.68 dB. Always convert to one reference
distance before you compare two horns.

## Worked examples

### Level at a distance

A horn kit rated 150 dB at 10 ft:

```console
$ trainhorn-calc spl 150 100
130.0 dB at 100 ft

$ trainhorn-calc spl 150 1000
110.0 dB at 1000 ft

$ trainhorn-calc spl 150 5280
95.5 dB at 5280 ft
```

Each tenfold step in distance removes 20 dB. At one mile (5280 ft) the free
field model gives about 95.5 dB.

### Adding air absorption

Train horn chords sit mostly between about 250 and 600 Hz. At 500 Hz,
absorption is about 1.9 dB/km at 10 °C and 70 % relative humidity:

```console
$ trainhorn-calc spl 150 5280 --absorption 1.9
92.5 dB at 5280 ft

$ trainhorn-calc spl 120 50 --ref-distance 1 --unit m --absorption 1.9
85.9 dB at 50 m
```

Absorption costs about 3 dB at one mile but only 0.1 dB at 50 m: it matters at
long range only. The library ships rounded octave-band values in
`AIR_ABSORPTION_DB_PER_KM`:

| Octave band (Hz) | 63 | 125 | 250 | 500 | 1000 | 2000 | 4000 | 8000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| dB/km | 0.1 | 0.4 | 1.0 | 1.9 | 3.7 | 9.7 | 32.8 | 117.0 |

These are typical figures for 10 °C and 70 % RH (a condition tabulated in
ISO 9613-2, computed from the ISO 9613-1 equations). They change a lot with
temperature and humidity.

### Distance for a target level

The inverse problem: where does the 150 dB (at 10 ft) horn fall to 90 dB?

```console
$ trainhorn-calc distance 150 90
90 dB is reached at about 10,000.0 ft

$ trainhorn-calc distance 150 90 --absorption 1.9
90 dB is reached at about 6,491.3 ft

$ trainhorn-calc distance 120 85 --ref-distance 1 --unit m
85 dB is reached at about 56.2 m
```

Without absorption the closed form is `d = d_ref·10^((L_ref - L)/20)`. With
absorption there is no elementary closed form, so `distance_for_spl` solves it
by bisection; the level falls strictly with distance, so the root is unique.

### Two or more horns

Levels of independent (incoherent) sources add as powers, not as decibels:

```text
L = 10·log10( Σ 10^(L_i / 10) )
```

```console
$ trainhorn-calc combine 150 150
153.0 dB combined

$ trainhorn-calc combine 150 147
151.8 dB combined

$ trainhorn-calc combine 118 118 118
122.8 dB combined
```

Two equal horns add about 3 dB, three add about 4.8 dB.

## Python API

```python
from trainhorn_calc import (
    spl_at_distance, distance_for_spl, db_doubling_loss,
    distance_ratio_loss, combine_levels,
)

spl_at_distance(150, 100)                                    # 130.0
spl_at_distance(150, 5280, absorption_db_per_km=1.9)         # 92.49535914932376
spl_at_distance(120, 50, ref_distance=1, unit="m",
                absorption_db_per_km=1.9)                    # 85.92749991327962
distance_for_spl(150, 90)                                    # 10000.0
distance_for_spl(150, 90, absorption_db_per_km=1.9)          # 6491.256148481227
db_doubling_loss()                                           # 6.020599913279624
distance_ratio_loss(10)                                      # 20.0
combine_levels([150, 150])                                   # 153.0102999566398
```

## Limitations

- **Free field only.** No ground reflection, buildings, barriers, wind or
  temperature gradients. Real outdoor levels can be several dB higher or lower
  than the model, and more so at long range.
- **Point source.** Very close to the horn (within about one or two horn
  lengths) the law does not hold. The formula still returns a number
  (`spl_at_distance(150, 5)` gives 156.0), but treat anything inside the rated
  distance as unreliable.
- **Ratings vary.** Published horn dB figures are often peak values measured
  under conditions you cannot check. The output can only be as good as the
  input.
- **Not a measurement or a legal assessment.** Use a calibrated sound level
  meter for real numbers and check local noise rules separately.
